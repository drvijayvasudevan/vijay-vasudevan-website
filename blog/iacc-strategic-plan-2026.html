The IACC's New Autism Plan Asks the Right Questions. Will Anyone Be Accountable for the Answers?

VV
Vijay Vasudevan, Ph.D., M.P.H.
6 min read

On August 27, the Interagency Autism Coordinating Committee (IACC) voted to adopt its 2026–2028 Strategic Plan. If you don't follow federal autism policy, here's why it matters: the IACC advises the Secretary of Health and Human Services on autism research, services, and policy, and its strategic plan is the closest thing the country has to a national autism agenda. Under the Autism CARES Act of 2024, the plan also feeds directly into the budget estimate NIH sends to the President each year.

The plan arrived surrounded by controversy, much of it earned. But I think it's worth reading on two levels: what it says, and how it was made. Both matter for anyone who works in autism services, research, or public health.

What the plan gets right

Start with the substance, because some of it is overdue.

It takes the lifespan seriously. According to Reuters, the plan calls on federal agencies to nearly double their investment in autism, and not only in research. It reaches into medical care, housing, aging, and family support. For years, federal autism policy has acted as if autistic people stop existing at 21. This plan doesn't.

It counts adults. The plan calls for the federal government to estimate how many autistic adults there are, for the first time. Think about that. We have spent decades tracking autism prevalence in 8-year-olds and almost nothing on the millions of autistic adults who need health care, housing, and jobs. You can't plan services for a population you've never counted.

It focuses on health, not just behavior. The plan pushes agencies to improve how we assess and treat the conditions that commonly come with autism: GI problems, sleep disruption, seizures, and mental health conditions. These drive emergency visits, hospitalizations, and early death. They deserve more attention than they've gotten.

It names profound autism. For the first time, the strategic plan includes a focus on profound autism. Families of people with the highest support needs have long said they were left out of research and policy. That critique was fair.

It measures what's actually delivered. One detail I hope survives implementation: the housing and supported living section proposes tracking direct-support hours that are authorized, scheduled, delivered, and left unfilled. Very few state systems make that distinction. A service that's approved on paper but never shows up isn't a service. This is exactly the kind of outcome tracking that autism services have been missing.

Where it falls short

The definition problem. Definitions are policy. Who gets counted determines who gets served. The IACC adopted its own functional definition of profound autism rather than waiting for research consensus, and some committee members warned that a narrow boundary could shut people out of studies. A label meant to make a population visible can also become a gate. If states or agencies start using it to decide eligibility, people just outside the line could lose access. And many autistic advocates object to the term itself.

The money may not follow. The plan recommends about $747 million a year by fiscal year 2028, up from the roughly $390 million Congress currently authorizes. But it's a recommendation, not an appropriation. Earlier IACC plans called for $685 million and never got close. Meanwhile, federal agencies named in the plan filed dozens of corrections saying they lacked the expertise or funding to take on the roles assigned to them. A plan that assigns work to agencies that haven't agreed to it is a wish list, not a roadmap.

It's harder to track progress. The plan drops the seven-question structure the IACC had used since 2009 and puts less emphasis on genetic research. Whatever you think of that shift, abandoning the old framework makes it much harder to compare this plan to earlier ones or to see whether anything improved.

The process problem

This is where the plan loses credibility it didn't need to lose.

The draft, a 336-page document, came out in July with only four days for public comment. After pushback, HHS extended the window to August 20. The chair reported more than 5,000 public comments. But several committee members said the last 4,100 pages of comments, nearly half the total, weren't delivered until the day before the vote. Federal staff acknowledged they could only look at recurring themes because they didn't have the staff to fully review the volume.

The vote itself was telling. Reuters reported that the public members voted yes, while many federal agency representatives abstained, with some saying they needed more time to review the plan and comments. When a federal member raised concerns that the process was inaccessible, especially for people with slower processing, and pointed to the committee's obligations under Sections 501 and 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, those concerns were largely dismissed. An autism committee that can't make its own process accessible to autistic people has a problem.

Then, less than a month later, Secretary Kennedy replaced the chair who steered the plan through with Dr. John Gaitanis, a pediatric neurologist and the new head of NIH's child health institute. HHS said a federal chair would help with implementation. Maybe. But a pediatric focus at the top raises a fair question: will the plan's adult and aging priorities hold up?

What I'm watching

I don't think the right response to this plan is to dismiss it. Several of its priorities, including adult prevalence, co-occurring health conditions, and delivered versus authorized services, are ones disability researchers have been asking for. The right response is to hold it to its own promises:

  1. Does the adult prevalence estimate actually get funded and done?
  2. Does the profound autism definition stay a research tool, or does it become an eligibility gate?
  3. Do agencies report progress against specific goals? The Government Accountability Office has already found that past IACC plans described progress without tying it to goals.
  4. Does a plain-language version ever come out? Advocates asked for one. A national autism plan that most autistic people and families can't read isn't finished.
Public health has a habit of treating plans as accomplishments. They aren't.

The measure of this one will be what changes for an autistic adult trying to see a doctor, keep a support worker, or find a place to live in 2028.
